Haptics

Touch as the interface.

The rail renders a lighter, damped panel under your hand, and stays passive while doing it.

There is no force sensor in the handle. A momentum observer compares what the motor is doing with how the carriage moves and attributes the difference to a hand. The controller then renders an admittance, Mv·ẍ + Bv·ẋ = F̂hand + effects, with a virtual mass lighter than the real one, plus detents and a soft close. Give it a push and let go, and the gesture is read as intent: the carriage finishes the move under the energy governor. Hold it back, and the obstruction detector stops it (a drawer releases to zero force, a door or wall backs off 50 mm). Rendered lightness never reduces impact energy: the governor and the monitor act on the rated mass.

How to touch it. Drag the panel with a mouse or a finger: your pointer pulls the handle through a virtual hand spring (drawer 1.5 N/mm, at most 30 N; door 2 N/mm, 60 N; wall 4 N/mm, 150 N), and the engine receives only that force, as it would receive a hand's. A mouse cannot feel the panel pull away, so when the controller reads your gesture and takes over the move, the page lets go of the handle for you, as a hand would; grab the moving panel and hold still to hold it back. Or focus the stage and hold ← / → to push (add Shift for a firm push); O, C and S send Open, Close and Stop. A seated drawer opens when you push it in firmly (about 20 N, 1.5 mm into its spring push-stop) and let go; pulling a seated drawer does nothing, because an outward force at the seat reads as cabinet pressure.

Passivity: why the virtual spring has a ceiling

A virtual spring is rendered by a sampled loop: the controller reads the position, waits one period, and holds a force for another. Each lag leaks a little energy into the carriage every cycle. Colgate and Schenkel's bound says the device's own damping b must outrun that leak: K < Kmax = 2b/(T + 2Td). Above it, a light touch starts an oscillation that grows. fabrail keeps its rendered springs at 0.3·2b/T and runs a Hannaford–Ryu passivity controller on top: it watches the energy the loop has put out and adds just enough damping to take it back.

This lab is a JavaScript sandbox that mirrors core::haptics::passivity on a 1-D mass with the class's physical damping and fast-loop period. The WebAssembly controller cannot be driven into this case: its passivity controller is always on and params::validate rejects a spring above Kmax. That is how the unstable case is shown safely.

What this page shows, and what it does not

  • Live, in WebAssembly: the playground runs the Rust observer, intent detector, admittance, detents, terminus, governor, obstruction detector and supervisor (fabrail-core through fabrail-rt) on the Carriage6 rigid-body plant, stepped in 1 ms slices in your browser. The hand is a pure force source on the front, which is all the engine's input carries.
  • Your hand is modelled. A mouse cannot push back, so the page converts your pointer into a force with a virtual spring and damper. Holding the pointer still while the panel moves is, to the controller, a hand holding it back.
  • The apparent-mass readout is indicative: a least-squares fit of F − Bv·v = M·a over the last half second of hand-driven motion, using the plant's truth acceleration. FAB-HAP-001 (±10 % of Mv) is verified by its own push profile in the test suite.
  • Without the engine the playground falls back to a JavaScript copy of the admittance law with the detent and soft-close spring and says so; there is no intent, obstruction or supervisor in that fallback.
  • The passivity lab is a sandbox, labelled illustrative. No hardware haptic device was measured; no feel is claimed beyond what the simulation shows.
